Surround Sound Test: Checking 5.1 and 7.1 Home Theatre Channels

A step-by-step guide to testing 5.1 and 7.1 surround setups, identifying swapped or silent channels and getting the subwoofer and centre speaker right.

Why surround setups go wrong

A 5.1 system has five full-range speakers and a subwoofer; a 7.1 system adds two more surrounds. That is six or eight cables, each of which can be swapped, loose or connected to the wrong terminal. Receivers also have dozens of settings for speaker size, distance and level. It is no surprise that many home theatres run for years with a centre speaker that is too quiet or rear channels that are reversed.

A channel test is the fastest way to find these problems, because it plays a sound through one speaker at a time and you simply confirm where it came from.

Speaker layout at a glance

In a 5.1 layout, front left and front right sit either side of the screen, the centre sits directly above or below it, and the surround left and right sit beside or slightly behind the listening position. The subwoofer can go almost anywhere, though placement strongly affects bass quality. A 7.1 layout adds rear left and rear right behind the listener.

The centre channel carries most dialogue, so if voices are hard to understand, that speaker deserves the most attention.

Running the test

Open a 5.1 and 7.1 surround sound test on a device connected to your receiver or soundbar, usually over HDMI. Make sure the output is set to multichannel rather than stereo in the operating system and that the receiver is not forcing a stereo mode. Play each channel in turn and note any that are silent, swapped or noticeably louder or quieter.

Not every browser and device passes more than two channels. If every speaker plays the same sound, the source is probably sending stereo that the receiver is upmixing.

Fixing common problems

Swapped channels are almost always cables. Trace each wire from the receiver terminal to the speaker. A silent channel may be disconnected, set to None in the receiver's speaker configuration, or connected to the wrong zone output. A centre speaker that sounds weak can usually be raised by a few decibels in the channel level settings.

If the subwoofer is silent, check its power, its own volume knob and its crossover setting, and confirm the receiver has the subwoofer enabled. If bass sounds thin with everything running, the subwoofer may be out of phase with the mains; try its phase switch.

Calibrating levels

Most receivers include automatic calibration with a microphone, which is a good starting point. Fine-tune by ear afterwards: dialogue should be clear without riding the volume, and surround effects should be noticeable but not distracting.

Phase matters too. If the front speakers sound vague, a speaker polarity test can show whether one is wired in reverse.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a special source for surround tests? You need a playback chain that can output more than two channels. Many computers can do this over HDMI to a receiver, provided the operating system is configured for 5.1 or 7.1 output. Some soundbars accept only stereo or compressed formats from a computer, which limits what a browser test can show.

Why does dialogue sound quiet compared with effects? This is usually a centre channel level issue or a dynamic range setting. Raise the centre by two or three decibels, or enable a night or dialogue mode if your receiver offers one.

Where should the subwoofer go? Corners give the most bass but often the boomiest. Try the subwoofer crawl: put the sub at your seat, play bass, move around the front of the room and place the sub where the bass sounds most even.

How often should I re-test? After moving furniture, adding speakers, changing cables or updating receiver firmware. Room changes affect bass and balance more than people expect.
